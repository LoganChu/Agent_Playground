Ten households where tax software is wrong

Each row below is one household, computed twice: the answer the statute gives, and the answer you get by reading the obvious thing instead. Both sides are computed in this page, by the same engine, from the same household — so nothing here is a figure somebody typed in, and none of it can go stale while the engine is right.

Run these ten against your own calculator. If it disagrees, the reason is in the row.

What is not here, and why

A row earns its place only if the wrong answer can be produced — by changing one input, one filing status or one year. Three of this project's sharpest findings cannot be, and they are named rather than dropped:

  • Arkansas's flat $287.30 needs a model built from the published bracket table alone, which this engine deliberately is not. What is reachable is the 13.70% marginal rate, which is in the table above.
  • Oklahoma's earned income credit is 5% of the federal credit as the Internal Revenue Code stood on 31 December 2020. No field in either package carries a frozen federal credit, which is why Oklahoma ships no credit rule at all rather than one that is a fifth too large.
  • Kansas's $2,320 exemption for a stillbirth turns on a fact no tax return records.

A page of findings that quietly left out the ones it cannot compute would look sharper and be worth less.

Where these come from

Every figure is computed by us-state-tax, a dependency-free engine covering 42 states and 1,033 local income taxes for tax years 2025 and 2026. Each state parameter is cited to the statute or state release it came from, in a ledger that records which document and what date — and the answers are compared against PolicyEngine-US, an independently built model, over 2,816 households on every push.

Nothing on this page is sent anywhere. The whole calculation runs in your browser.
